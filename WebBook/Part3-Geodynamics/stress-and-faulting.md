# Stress, Faults and the Mohr Circle

Faults slip when the stress on them exceeds what friction on the fault plane can hold. To say when that happens we need two things: a way to find the stress on any plane through a point in the rock, given the state of stress there, and a criterion for failure written in terms of that stress. This chapter builds both, and then uses them to explain why normal faults dip steeply, thrusts dip gently, and strike-slip faults are vertical.

:::{note} Sign convention
Throughout this chapter **compressive stress is positive**, as is usual in geology and rock mechanics. Much of continuum mechanics takes tension as positive; the equations below then change only in sign.
:::

## Stress on a plane

Cut a body under load along a plane and something must replace the material removed to keep the rest in equilibrium. That something is a force per unit area acting on the cut, the **traction** $\mathbf{T}$. It depends on the orientation of the cut, which we describe by the unit normal $\mathbf{n}$. All the tractions on all the planes through a point are generated by one object, the stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$:

```{math}
:label: eq-cauchy-traction
T_i = \sum_j \sigma_{ij} n_j, \qquad \mathbf{T} = \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{n}
```

In three dimensions $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ has nine components, $\sigma_{ij}$ being the $i$ component of the traction on the plane whose normal points along $j$. Balancing the moments on a small cube shows that the tensor is symmetric, $\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji}$, which leaves six independent components.

A fault does not feel the whole tensor. It feels the traction on its own plane, and that traction has two parts that matter: the **normal stress** $\sigma_n$ pressing the two sides together, and the **shear stress** $\tau$ along the plane, which tends to make them slide:

```{math}
:label: eq-normal-shear
\sigma_n = \mathbf{n} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{n}, \qquad
\tau = \mathbf{f} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{n}
```

where $\mathbf{f}$ is a unit vector in the plane ([](#fig-traction-on-a-plane)).

### How does this look in practice?

Suppose that in a map-view (two-dimensional) problem the stress is

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{xx} & \sigma_{xy} \\ \sigma_{xy} & \sigma_{yy} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 40 & 10 \\ 10 & 60 \end{bmatrix} \ \textrm{MPa}
$$

and we want the stress on a fault striking at $45°$ to the east. Its normal is $\mathbf{n} = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$ and a unit vector along it is $\mathbf{f} = (-1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$. The traction is

$$
\mathbf{T} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 50 \\ 70 \end{bmatrix} \ \textrm{MPa},
$$

and projecting it onto $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{f}$ gives $\sigma_n = 60$ MPa and $\tau = 10$ MPa. Of the three numbers in the tensor, these two are all the fault knows about.

## Principal stresses and invariants

Turn the plane and $\sigma_n$ and $\tau$ change. For a particular set of mutually perpendicular orientations the shear stress vanishes and the traction is a pure push along the normal. These are the **principal directions**, and the normal stresses on them are the **principal stresses**, ordered $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \sigma_3$ (with compression positive, $\sigma_1$ is the most compressive). Finding them is the eigenvalue problem for $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$: in the principal frame the tensor is diagonal,

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma} \;\longrightarrow\; \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma_3 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

For the example above the principal stresses are $50 \pm 10\sqrt{2}$ MPa, that is $64.1$ and $35.9$ MPa, with the most compressive direction at $67.5°$ to $x$.

Some combinations of the components do not change when the axes are rotated. In two dimensions the trace and the determinant are such **invariants**: for the example, $\sigma_{xx} + \sigma_{yy} = 100$ MPa and $\sigma_{xx}\sigma_{yy} - \sigma_{xy}^2 = 2300$ MPa$^2$ in map coordinates, and the same values follow from the principal stresses. In three dimensions there are three,

$$
I_1 = \sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_3, \qquad
I_2 = \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2\sigma_3 + \sigma_3\sigma_1, \qquad
I_3 = \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 ,
$$

and $I_1 = \sigma_{xx} + \sigma_{yy} + \sigma_{zz}$ and $I_3 = \det\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ in any frame. Whether a rock breaks cannot depend on how we chose our axes, so any failure criterion must be a function of the invariants alone.

The first invariant gives the **mean stress**, $\sigma_m = I_1/3$, which in the Earth is usually the pressure. Subtracting it leaves the **deviatoric stress**,

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \sigma_m \mathbf{I} + \boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime},
$$

which carries all the shear. Pressure alone compresses rock without distorting it; it is the deviatoric part that drives faulting and flow.

## The Mohr circle

How do $\sigma_n$ and $\tau$ vary as the plane turns? Work in the plane containing $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$, and let $\theta$ be the angle between the normal to the plane and the $\sigma_1$ direction. Resolving the forces on a wedge-shaped element and using the double-angle identities gives

```{math}
:label: eq-mohr-components
\sigma_n = \frac{\sigma_1 + \sigma_3}{2} + \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_3}{2}\cos 2\theta, \qquad
\tau = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_3}{2}\sin 2\theta .
```

Eliminating $\theta$ with $\cos^2 2\theta + \sin^2 2\theta = 1$,

```{math}
:label: eq-mohr-circle
\left[\sigma_n - \tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_1 + \sigma_3)\right]^2 + \tau^2 = \left[\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)\right]^2 .
```

This is a circle in the $(\sigma_n, \tau)$ plane, centred on the mean of $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$ with radius half their difference ([](#fig-mohr-circle)). Every plane through the point is represented by one point on it. Culmann derived the construction in the 1860s for uniaxial load and Mohr generalised it; the difference $\sigma_1 - \sigma_3$, the circle's diameter, is the **differential stress**.

Two features of the construction are worth holding on to. Angles on the circle are **doubled**: a plane at $\theta$ to $\sigma_1$ in the rock lies at $2\theta$ round the circle from the point $\sigma_1$. And the shear stress is greatest, equal to the radius, on planes at $45°$ to $\sigma_1$.

For the worked example, the centre is at $50$ MPa and the radius is $10\sqrt{2} \approx 14.1$ MPa; the $45°$ fault plots at $(60, 10)$ MPa, which lies on the circle. In three dimensions there are three circles, one for each pair of principal stresses; all possible planes plot on or between them, and the largest, through $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$, contains the planes most likely to fail.

## When does a fault slip?

Laboratory experiments on rock show that a plane slips, or a fresh fracture forms, when the shear stress on it reaches a value that rises linearly with the normal stress across it. This is the **Coulomb criterion**,

```{math}
:label: eq-coulomb
\tau = C + \mu\,\sigma_n = C + \sigma_n \tan\phi ,
```

where $C$ is the cohesion, $\mu$ the coefficient of friction and $\phi$ the angle of internal friction. For most rocks $\mu$ lies between about $0.4$ and $0.85$. On the Mohr diagram the criterion is a straight line, the **failure envelope**: states whose circle stays below it are stable, and failure begins when the circle first touches it ([](#fig-mohr-coulomb)).

How far a given plane is from failure is measured by the **Coulomb failure function**,

$$
\mathrm{CFF} = \tau - (C + \mu\,\sigma_n),
$$

the vertical gap between the plane's point and the envelope: negative while the plane is stable, zero when it slips. An earthquake on one fault changes the stress on its neighbours, and the change

$$
\Delta\mathrm{CFF} = \Delta\tau - \mu\,\Delta\sigma_n
$$

tells us whether a neighbouring fault was brought closer to failure ($\Delta\mathrm{CFF} > 0$) or moved away from it, into a *stress shadow*. The two terms can act against each other: a slip that reduces the shear on a neighbour but also unclamps it can still move it towards failure.

### Which plane fails?

The circle touches the envelope at the point where the radius is perpendicular to it. The envelope rises at the angle $\phi$, so that radius makes an angle $90° + \phi$ with the $\sigma_n$ axis, measured from $\sigma_1$. Halving the angle,

```{math}
:label: eq-failure-angle
2\theta = 90° + \phi \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \theta = 45° + \frac{\phi}{2} .
```

Since $\theta$ is measured to the normal, the fault plane itself lies at $45° - \phi/2$ to $\sigma_1$. For $\mu = 0.6$, $\phi \approx 31°$ and faults form at about $30°$ to $\sigma_1$. The circle touches the envelope at two points, symmetric about the $\sigma_n$ axis, so faults form in **conjugate pairs** on either side of $\sigma_1$.

The plane of greatest shear stress, at $45°$, is not the one that fails: friction resists slip in proportion to the normal stress, and the normal stress is smaller on planes more nearly parallel to $\sigma_1$.

## Anderson's theory of faulting

The Earth's surface carries no shear stress, so near the surface one principal stress must be vertical and the other two horizontal. Anderson (1905) combined this with the failure angle {eq}`eq-failure-angle` to predict the three classes of fault ([](#fig-anderson-faults)):

| Regime | Vertical principal stress | Fault plane | Dip |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | $\sigma_1$ | $30°$ from vertical | about $60°$ |
| Thrust (reverse) | $\sigma_3$ | $30°$ from horizontal | about $30°$ |
| Strike-slip | $\sigma_2$ | vertical, striking $30°$ from $\sigma_1$ | $90°$ |

The angles assume $\phi \approx 30°$. The spread of friction coefficients, $\phi$ between about $22°$ and $40°$, puts normal faults between about $56°$ and $65°$ and thrusts between $25°$ and $34°$. Read in reverse, the dip of a fault records the orientation of the stress that formed it, provided the fault has not been rotated since.

The same reasoning applies wherever a surface cannot carry shear. Topography rotates the principal directions near the surface, and a weak, slipping fault does the same in the rock around it, so that the principal directions bend to meet it.

## Pressure in the Earth and the effect of fluids

The weight of the overlying rock sets a reference state of stress. At depth $z$ beneath rock of density $\rho$ the vertical stress is the **lithostatic** stress,

$$
\sigma_v = \rho g z ,
$$

about $26$ MPa per kilometre for crustal rock ($\rho = 2700$ kg m$^{-3}$). If the horizontal stresses were equal to it the state would be isotropic and nothing would fail; tectonic loading adds a deviatoric part on top.

Fluid in the pores of a rock carries part of the load. The grains feel only the **effective stress**,

```{math}
:label: eq-effective-stress
\sigma_n^{\prime} = \sigma_n - p_f ,
```

where $p_f$ is the pore-fluid pressure. Raising $p_f$ lowers every normal stress by the same amount and leaves the shear stresses unchanged: the Mohr circle keeps its size and moves towards the envelope ([](#fig-mohr-coulomb)). This is how fluid injection can trigger earthquakes on faults that were already close to failure, and why high pore pressure allows thrust sheets to move on very gently dipping planes.
